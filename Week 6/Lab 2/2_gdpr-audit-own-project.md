Ian and Rea are addressed briefly. Colin gets the full treatment.

## Data Processing Brief

**Who is the client?**
Moinland is a small German coaching boutique. Two principals (CEO and CRO) coach early-stage tech startup founders. They have no dedicated legal or compliance team.

**What personal data does the system process?**

- **Colin:** Full coaching session transcripts generated by Granola (an AI note-taking app). These contain names, job titles, company names, professional challenges, and personal statements made by coaching clients in a confidential context. Health-adjacent content (stress, burnout, personal crises) may appear.
- **Ian:** A short Telegram message from the CEO (the topic idea). This is the CEO's own data — no third-party personal data.
- **Rea:** Public RSS feed articles. No personal data.

The meaningful personal data risk sits entirely with Colin.

**Where does the data come from?**
Granola automatically transcribes coaching calls (Zoom/Meet). The CEO or CRO saves the transcript to a Google Drive folder. Colin picks it up from there.

**What is the data used for?**
1. Extract discussion topics from the session.
2. Store the session as vector embeddings in Pinecone for future context retrieval.
3. Generate a LinkedIn post draft based on the topics.

**Who processes it?**
Moinland (controller) → n8n (automation layer) → OpenAI API (topic extraction and post generation) → Pinecone (vector storage) → Notion (draft output storage). Granola creates the transcript. Google Drive stores the raw file.

**Where is data stored and processed?**
All vendors are US-based. Data leaves the EU at every step after the Drive folder. No processing happens on EU servers.

**Does the system make decisions that affect people?**
No. Colin generates a draft post for the CEO/CRO to review. No coaching client is scored, ranked, or flagged. No automated decisions with legal or significant personal effects are made about any data subject.

## Phase 3: Lawful Basis Assessment

| Purpose | Proposed lawful basis | One-line justification | Flag for legal review? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Topic extraction from transcripts for content drafting | Legitimate interests (Art. 6(1)(f)) | Moinland has a commercial interest in creating relevant content; however, clients likely do not expect their confidential session to feed a content machine. | **Yes** |
| Semantic storage of transcripts in Pinecone | TBD — legal review | Storing personal data in a US vector database for indefinite future querying is hard to justify under LI without a strong balancing case. Consent may be required. | **Yes** |
| Processing CEO Telegram message (Ian) | Legitimate interests | The CEO is processing their own data for their own business purpose. Low risk. | No |

**Legitimate Interests Assessment (LIA) — Topic extraction:**

1. **Is the interest legitimate?** Yes — Moinland wants to create LinkedIn content that reflects real coaching conversations. This is a genuine business need.
2. **Is the processing necessary?** Partially. Sending the full transcript to OpenAI is more than necessary. Only anonymized topic summaries are needed. The current design fails the necessity test.
3. **Does the individual's interest override?** Unclear, but risky. Coaching clients share things in confidence. The relationship implies confidentiality. Clients likely have a reasonable expectation that their session content stays between them and the coach. This creates a significant risk that the balancing test tips against Moinland. **Flag for legal review.**

## Phase 4: Risk and Rights Analysis

**Special-category data (Article 9)**

Health-adjacent content — stress, burnout, depression, physical health mentioned in the context of work performance — may appear in coaching transcripts. The GDPR considers health data special-category. If such content is present, Moinland needs an Article 9(2) condition in addition to an Article 6 basis. The most plausible condition would be **explicit consent** (Art. 9(2)(a)) — which does not currently exist. This is a significant gap. Moinland should assume special-category data may be present until transcript content is reviewed and a policy is established.

**Automated decision-making (Article 22)**

No. The system produces a draft LinkedIn post for human review. No decision is made about any coaching client. Article 22 does not apply.

**DPIA trigger — EDPB nine criteria:**

| Criterion | Applies? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Evaluation or scoring of people | No | Clients are not scored or profiled |
| Automated decision-making with significant effects | No | Only content drafts are produced |
| Systematic monitoring | Partial | All sessions are processed and stored — systematic collection of client data |
| Special-category data | Yes | Health-adjacent content likely present |
| Large-scale processing | No | Small firm, low volume |
| Matching or combining datasets | Yes | Colin queries past sessions to add context — combining data across sessions |
| Data concerning vulnerable people | No | Startup founders are not a designated vulnerable group |
| Innovative technology | Yes | Generative AI + vector embeddings on confidential coaching data is novel |
| Cross-border transfer preventing rights exercise | Yes | All processors are US-based; erasure from Pinecone embeddings is technically complex |

**At least four criteria apply. A DPIA is required before Colin is used in production.**

**Data subject rights friction:**

- **Right of access (Art. 15):** A coaching client could ask what Moinland holds about them. There is no inventory linking a client's name to their embeddings in Pinecone, their transcript in Drive, or any topics extracted. Responding within 30 days would be very difficult today.
- **Right to erasure (Art. 17):** Deleting a client from Pinecone requires knowing which vectors correspond to their data — there is no metadata structure for this. Drive deletion is manual. There is no defined process.
- **Right to object (Art. 21):** If LI is the lawful basis, clients have the right to object. There is no mechanism for them to do so — no privacy notice, no contact point, no process.

## Phase 6: Compliance Memo

**TO:** Legal Counsel / Data Protection Officer, Moinland
**FROM:** Ironhack AI Consulting Team
**RE:** GDPR First-Pass Assessment — Colin Agent (Coaching to LinkedIn)
**DATE:** May 2026

---

**Bottom line: Proceed with conditions.**

The Colin agent has a real and useful purpose. The privacy problems are fixable — but the system cannot go live as currently built. Coaching session transcripts contain personal data of your clients (and possibly health-adjacent data), they are processed by US-based vendors without Data Processing Agreements in place, and there is no lawful basis documented, no client transparency, and no way to respond to data subject rights requests. These are concrete GDPR obligations, not optional best practices.

**Top three actions, in order:**

1. **Sign Data Processing Agreements before any transcript data flows.** You are sending personal data to OpenAI, Pinecone, Google Drive, and Notion. Each of these requires a signed DPA under Article 28 GDPR — this is not optional. Google Workspace and OpenAI API both offer standard DPAs. For Pinecone and Granola, check their documentation today. If a vendor cannot provide an adequate DPA and transfer mechanism (SCCs or DPF certification), do not use them for this data.

2. **Conduct a DPIA before deployment.** At least four EDPB criteria apply to Colin's processing: special-category data (health content), systematic monitoring, combining datasets across sessions, and cross-border transfers. This legally triggers a DPIA. Engage legal counsel to conduct it. The DPIA will also determine whether legitimate interests holds as your lawful basis, or whether you need explicit consent from coaching clients.

3. **Redesign Colin to minimize the data it sends to external vendors.** Instead of sending full transcript text to OpenAI and storing full content in Pinecone, send only anonymized topic summaries. This is technically straightforward and immediately reduces your privacy exposure. It also makes the legitimate interests balancing test significantly easier to pass.

**Residual risks (even if you follow these steps):**

- **Transcript content is unpredictable.** Clients may discuss health, legal disputes, or personal crises. Even with a DPIA and DPAs in place, you cannot fully control what appears in a transcript. A content review policy — deciding which session types are eligible for Colin processing — would reduce this risk but not eliminate it.
- **Granola's role is legally ambiguous.** Granola creates the transcript. Depending on their terms of service, they may process data for their own purposes (product improvement, model training), making them a joint controller rather than your processor. If so, your clients may have rights against Granola that you cannot fulfill on their behalf. This needs legal clarification before relying on Granola as part of a GDPR-compliant pipeline.
- **Erasure from vector databases is technically complex.** Even with a defined process, deleting a specific client's embeddings from Pinecone requires metadata tagging at ingestion time. This does not exist in the current design. Retrofitting this after data is already stored is harder than building it in from the start.

**What this memo is not:** This is not a legal opinion, a DPIA, or a certification. Do not rely on it for compliance decisions without engaging qualified legal counsel.

## Stretch: Data Protection by Design — Colin Transcript Processing

| Design principle | Current state | Pass / Fail / Unknown |
|---|---|---|
| Data minimisation | Full transcript text sent to OpenAI and stored in Pinecone. Only anonymized topic summaries are needed for the stated purpose. | **Fail** |
| Purpose binding | No technical controls prevent embeddings from being queried for future, undefined purposes. | **Fail** |
| Access controls | Pinecone index and Google Drive folder permissions not documented. Unknown who has access. | **Unknown** |
| Retention enforcement | No deletion policy. Transcripts and embeddings stored indefinitely. | **Fail** |
| Subject rights workflow | No process to identify, retrieve, or delete a specific client's data across all systems within 30 days. | **Fail** |
| Incident response | No documented breach detection or 72-hour notification procedure. | **Fail** |

**What needs to change:**

The root fix is **data minimisation**. If Colin extracts topics on-device or in n8n before calling OpenAI — and sends only a structured, anonymized topic list rather than raw transcript text — then Pinecone stores no personal data at all, the transfer risk drops to near zero, and most of the checklist items become significantly easier. This is one engineering decision that closes multiple compliance gaps at once.
